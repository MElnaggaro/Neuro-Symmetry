03 · System2 min

Four stages, end to end.

01

Capture & Quality Gate

MediaPipe FaceMesh (468 landmarks) with Kalman + EMA smoothing. Frames rejected for blur, occlusion, low luminance, or yaw > 25°.

02

Feature Extraction

Nose-centered, IPD-scaled normalization. 50-D vector: 40 mirror-distance pairs + EAR + brow/mouth asymmetry + texture.

03

Classification

SymmetryNetV2: BatchNorm → SE → Linear(50→128) → TokenAttention → Linear(128→64→32) → ArcFace head → softmax over Normal / Mild / Severe.

04

Temporal Engine

EMA (α=0.10) + 60-frame trajectory regression + 90-frame z-score change detector + 5-frame confirmation FSM.

Stage one is browser-side capture. MediaPipe FaceMesh produces 468 dense landmarks per frame; we apply Kalman smoothing in the FaceLandmarker, then a second EMA pass to attenuate slow drift. Quality gates reject frames that fall outside our operating envelope — luminance below 40, Laplacian blur variance below 40, frontal occlusion, or head yaw past 25 degrees. Stage two — and this is the important one — we don't feed the image to the network. We extract a 50-dimensional engineered vector: 40 bilateral mirror-pair distances, six eye-aspect-ratio terms, brow and mouth asymmetry scalars, and texture and symmetry error scores. All of it normalized to a nose-centered, inter-pupillary-distance-scaled coordinate frame so it's comparable across faces and cameras. Stage three is the classifier — SymmetryNetV2, the architecture I'll detail next. Stage four is post-hoc temporal logic: an exponential moving average, a 60-frame linear regression for trajectory, a z-score change detector against a 90-frame baseline, and a five-frame finite-state machine that prevents single-frame false alarms from being raised as confirmed events.

03 · System

SymmetryNetV2 — the actual architecture.

Input 50-D engineered feature vector·single frame
↓
Pre-proc BatchNorm1d·SE block (50→25→50, sigmoid gate)
↓
Backbone Linear(50→128)·TokenAttention 4-head·Linear(128→64→32)
↓
Heads ArcFace margin (m=0.45, s=30)·Linear(32→3) logits
↓
Training FocalLoss γ=2.0·ArcFace 60/40·HardNegativeMiner
↓
Post-hoc Temperature scaling (LBFGS)·EMA + trajectory + z-score·5-frame FSM·region attribution

No CNN. No end-to-end deep learning. Engineered features by design — clinically interpretable, auditable against anatomical priors. ~42K parameters · ~105 KB ONNX · CPU-only inference.

This is a deliberate design choice and worth defending directly. The 50-D feature vector is already extracted and geometrically normalized before the network sees it — so this is a tabular classification problem on structured anatomical geometry, not pixel-level scene understanding. The Squeeze-and-Excitation block recalibrates per-feature importance with a sigmoid gate. The four-head Token Attention layer learns relationships across the feature sequence and projects each token into an 8-dimensional embedding. The ArcFace head adds an angular margin of 0.45 radians in embedding space — that's a face-recognition technique, used here to push Normal, Mild, and Severe further apart. During training, FocalLoss with gamma 2.0 handles class imbalance, the Hard Negative Miner rebuilds every 15 epochs to oversample the hardest Normal samples near the decision boundary, and a single LBFGS-fitted temperature scalar brings expected calibration error to about 0.002. Roughly 42 thousand parameters, 105 kilobytes on disk, runs CPU-only. The intelligence is distributed across modules — that's what makes it auditable.

The numbers are real and measured — F1 macro 0.986, AUC 0.998, sensitivity 0.982, expected calibration error 0.0018 — from the metrics_v2.json file written by the V2 training run on a 462,668-sample internal test split. I want to be precise about what that means. Why are they plausible? Because tabular classification on 50 normalized geometric features over a constrained symmetry decision is a much narrower problem than pixel-level scene understanding. The Hard Negative Miner and FocalLoss directly attack the class-imbalance shortcut, ArcFace margin pushes the classes apart in embedding space, and temperature scaling drops calibration error to near-zero. But — and this is the part that matters for academic scrutiny — the training corpus was CelebA for normal faces, 300-W for landmark supervision, and the YFP dataset's regional patches for palsy examples. Roughly 14,000 eye, eyebrow, and mouth crops drawn from 22 facial-palsy patients. There were no stroke-patient images in training. AffectNet is referenced in our training script but the directory wasn't present on disk during this run. So this is a strong internal-test result on a narrow distribution. It is not yet a claim about real-world clinical performance, and I won't overstate it as one.

Real-world conditions?

Quality gates reject low luminance (HSV V<40), motion blur (Laplacian variance<40), occlusion (z-spread<0.01), and extreme yaw (>25°). Appendix · Credibility & Risk

What survives scrutiny — and what doesn't.

Why the architecture is defensible

  • Tabular structured problem; pre-normalized geometric features
  • Engineered features map directly to anatomical priors (mirror pairs, EAR, asymmetry scalars)
  • 50-D input · ~42K parameters → very low memorization risk vs. a CNN of similar accuracy
  • Temperature scaling + ECE 0.0018 → probabilities are usable, not just argmax
  • 60/40 FocalLoss/ArcFace + HardNegativeMiner directly target shortcut learning
  • Post-hoc temporal FSM separates single-frame noise from confirmed events

Risks that remain

  • Single-source training distribution (no real stroke cohort)
  • Calibration tuned on the same distribution as test — degrades on demographic / camera shift
  • assumed_fps=30 constant baked into onset-time math
  • No liveness detection; no anti-spoofing layer
  • 5-frame confirmation FSM resets on 3-frame WebSocket dropout
  • Personalized baseline removed in V2 — pre-existing asymmetry will be misread
  • Insensitive to subtle ptosis without EAR delta and to nasolabial flattening

Built for the seconds that matter — and honest about the seconds it can't yet account for. Source: ai/checkpoints/metrics_v2.json · Neuro-v2-Reprot.md §9 · ai/v2/model_v2.py · ai/v2/losses.py
